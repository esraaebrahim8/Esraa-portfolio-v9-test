Judgement under pressure, rehearsed safely

01

Context

When a payments platform goes down, it loses money and customer trust by the minute. The people who run those incidents are often doing it for the first time — and no runbook prepares you for the pressure of a live outage.

New responders don't fail because they lack knowledge. They freeze: going quiet when they should speak, or flooding executives when they should be shielding the team fixing the problem. That's a judgement gap, and a quiz can't close it.

So I built a place to rehearse the judgement — a decision game where you run a real outage and live with the consequences of every call.

02

Learning Need & Objectives

Learning Need
New incident leads know the runbook but hesitate on the calls that matter — when to escalate, when to wait, who to tell first.
Target Audience
New and developing operations staff moving into incident-lead responsibilities.
  • Prioritise the next right action when several things are on fire at once.
  • Communicate at the right moment, to the right stakeholder.
  • Protect the team fixing the problem from unnecessary interruption.

04

Process

I started from the behaviours a good incident lead actually shows under pressure, then worked backwards into a scenario that forces each one.

Frame

Define the behaviours

Prioritising, communicating at the right moment, and protecting the team — written as things you can watch someone do.

→
Build

Branch the scenario

One outage, seven stages, eight endings — every path shaped by the learner's calls, not a fixed script.

→
Close

Debrief the decisions

An executive report that turns the run into reflection, then invites a replay.

05

Key Decisions

The instructional choices that shaped the experience:

A game, not a course

Judgement transfers through practice and consequence. Playing the role builds instinct that reading never will.

No correct answer shown

Real incidents don't reveal the right move. Living with each call is what makes the judgement stick.

Competing live metrics

Six signals pull in different directions, so learners feel the trade-offs instead of being told about them.

A clock, and a pause

The timer makes the pressure real; the pause keeps it humane. Pressure drives transfer; safety keeps people learning.

Feedback you can trust

Every point explains itself and every badge shows how it's earned — because feedback learners believe is feedback that changes behaviour.

Accessible by default

Extended-time and full keyboard control mean the challenge comes from the scenario, never the interface.

06

The Solution

A branching decision game built around consequence, not correction. Six live signals — customer trust, satisfaction, team workload, the learner's own stress, executive confidence, and reputation — respond to every choice, so the invisible trade-offs of incident response become visible in the moment.

It closes with a structured debrief framed as an executive report: a mission grade across response speed, communication, stakeholder satisfaction, and business impact, plus named strengths and areas to improve. Field notes throughout carry the transferable principles home — acknowledge before you have answers, silence reads as absence, protect the people doing the fixing.

07

Outcome & Tools

The result is a complete, self-contained onboarding experience that lets new responders practise high-stakes judgement before they ever face a real incident — and gives their managers a consistent way to build that judgement across a team.

Scenario & branching design Decision-making under pressure Consequence-based feedback Reflection & debrief Accessibility

What it reinforced. The strongest designs make consequences felt, not explained. Removing the "correct answer" was uncomfortable on purpose — and it's exactly what turns a scenario into judgement someone can carry into a real incident.
